    Assets — How Store Licenses Bind

    [[[appendices.assets]]]

    Overview

    [[[appendices.assets#overview]]]

    Background for the license design in [[assets#licenses]]: when the terms of a store item bind the buyer, and so what a record should keep. None of it was checked against a store's current terms, and none of it is legal advice. It is what the design was reasoned from in the 2026-11-18 session, written down so a later change to the design can be argued against it. A statement about one store is from memory until the terms page is read and the reading saved ([[assets#licenses]]).

    The grant is made when the item is acquired

    [[[appendices.assets#acquisition]]]

    An asset license is a contract formed when the account gets the item, whether by buying it or by claiming it free. It is not made when the files are downloaded.

    • Terms are usually perpetual. A marketplace license is typically written so that content already in a project keeps its license, because once the files are inside a shipped product nothing can switch them off.
    • New terms apply to new acquisitions unless the agreement the buyer accepted let the store change it unilaterally. Many store agreements do contain such a clause, so usually is as strong as this can be put.
    • Creative Commons is irrevocable by its own text. An author can stop offering an item; a copy already obtained keeps the license it came with.
    • A store that merges catalogs is the test case. When Epic folded the Unreal Engine Marketplace and Sketchfab's store into Fab in 2024, items acquired earlier were recalled as keeping the license they were acquired under, with only new acquisitions on the Fab licenses. The UE Marketplace label on 11 of the 1,568 items of the owner's hand-made catalog is consistent with that and proves nothing about the terms.

    What the store can still gate

    [[[appendices.assets#gate]]]

    A game and an asset differ less in the license than in what stands between the buyer and the files each time they are used.

    • A launcher or an account sits in the way of a game. It can refuse to start or update the game until a new agreement is accepted, and a closed account takes the library with it. DRM or online activation does the same at a lower level. A DRM-free installer already downloaded has none of this: the publisher can change the text and has no way to apply it to the copy.
    • An asset in a project has nothing in the way. Nothing checks in with the store, so changed terms have no practical way to reach it.
    • An asset not yet downloaded is the game's case. It exists for the buyer only through the account and the store's launcher. A listing that is pulled, an account that is closed or a store that shuts can end the access while the right survives on paper. Only downloading the items that matter protects them.

    Downloading later, and the terms accepted on the way

    [[[appendices.assets#download-later]]]

    The license is fixed at acquisition; the gate to the files is the store as it is on the day of the download. Signing in through the launcher may ask for the store's current terms. Whether accepting them changes the license of an item already owned depends on the new text: terms that only cover use of the store and the account leave the item's license alone, while terms that replace earlier licenses for the whole library put the buyer on the current one. Only the terms settle which.

    The working assumption the design takes is the safe one: for an item never downloaded, the store's current text is the one the buyer is bound by, because it is the only one certain to have been accepted. An earlier text is better for the buyer only if they can show they acquired under it and nothing since replaced it.

    What the record keeps because of this

    [[[appendices.assets#record-keeps]]]

    • Two dates say which text is held and since when. acquired is when the license was formed; read in the .rehuterms is the day each saved text was read ([[assets#licenses]]). A scan reads the terms on the day it runs, so for an item acquired years earlier the two differ, and nothing recovers the older text unless it was saved.
    • A saved text is evidence, not a cache. That is why a refresh never replaces it unasked: the text an item was acquired under may be the one that still applies, so it is not the tool's to overwrite.
    • The step before downloading is a refresh. Refresh License ([[assets#licenses]]) shows whether the terms changed since the saved text, and the owner decides whether to replace it.

    What Fab's AI tags mean

    [[[appendices.assets#ai-tags]]]

    Fab's EULA (§16(l), read 2026-11-18 from /i/eula/fab_eula) has exactly two provisions on AI, and defines both through tags on the listing. The library scan writes them as advertised_tags, prefixed as every tag it makes from a store field is ([[assets#block]]).

    • NoAI, written as ai-forbidden (the listing's isAiForbidden). Under a Standard License, NoAI content may not be used in datasets used by generative AI programs, in developing such programs, or as training input to them (also §6(vii)). Programs that only operate on the original content, classify it, or arrange existing content without making new content are outside that definition, so using AI tools on the file is not what the tag forbids.
    • What binds is the tag "at the time of your Transaction". The scan reads today's listing, so a tag added or dropped since the item was got shows the listing's present state, not the terms it was got under.
    • On a Creative Commons item the CC license governs; the clause is the Standard License's.
    • CreatedWithAI, written as ai-generated (the listing's isAiGenerated). A disclosure, not a restriction: Fab requires sellers to tag content a material part of which was made with generative AI. Upscaling or content-aware fill does not count.
    • isAiRestricted sits on the listing's seller (user), not on the item, and is not license data.
